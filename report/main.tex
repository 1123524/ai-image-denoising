\title{A Comparative Study of Classical Filters and Deep Learning for Image Denoising}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{1123524 \quad 1123505 \quad 1123506}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
% 150-250 words. Problem, what you did, key result, conclusion.
Image denoising aims to recover a clean image from a noisy observation and is a
fundamental low-level vision task. This work presents a systematic comparison
between five classical denoising techniques---Gaussian, median, and bilateral
filtering, Non-Local Means (NLM), and Block-Matching and 3D filtering (BM3D)---and
a deep convolutional neural network, DnCNN, trained with residual learning for
blind Gaussian denoising. All methods are evaluated under identical conditions on
grayscale (Set12, BSD68) and color (Kodak) images, across additive white Gaussian
noise of varying strength and salt-and-pepper noise, using PSNR, SSIM, and runtime.
Experiments show that BM3D attains the highest PSNR/SSIM on Gaussian noise
(e.g., 29.99~dB at $\sigma=25$ on Set12), with our blind DnCNN a close second
(29.23~dB) while running roughly $185\times$ faster at inference (0.06~s vs.\
11.0~s per image). For salt-and-pepper noise, the median filter remains the
strongest method (31.16~dB) and the Gaussian-trained DnCNN degrades sharply,
illustrating the limits of a learned model outside its training noise
distribution. We analyze the trade-offs between detail preservation,
generalization across noise levels, and computational cost, and discuss when
classical priors remain competitive with learned models.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
image denoising, DnCNN, BM3D, non-local means, convolutional neural network, PSNR, SSIM

\section{Introduction}
% ~2 pages. Motivate denoising; noise model y = x + n; sensor/low-light context;
% classical -> learned evolution; contributions as a bullet list.
Digital images are inevitably corrupted by noise during acquisition and
transmission. Photon shot noise, sensor thermal noise, and quantization all
contribute degradations that are especially severe under low-light or
high-ISO conditions. Formally, a noisy observation is modeled as $y = x + n$,
where $x$ is the latent clean image and $n$ the noise; denoising seeks an
estimate $\hat{x}$ that is close to $x$. Because the problem is ill-posed---many
clean images could explain the same observation---all methods rely on a prior
about what natural images look like, whether hand-crafted (smoothness, edge
preservation, self-similarity) or learned from data.

Denoising is also a critical pre-processing step for downstream vision tasks
such as segmentation, recognition, and medical image analysis, where residual
noise propagates into later stages. Over the past two decades the field has
evolved from simple local filters, through non-local and transform-domain
methods such as BM3D, to data-driven convolutional neural networks. This work
revisits that progression empirically: under one common protocol we ask how a
modern learned denoiser (DnCNN) compares with five representative classical
filters, not only in PSNR/SSIM but also in runtime and robustness to noise
types and levels it was not trained on.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A unified, reproducible benchmark comparing five classical filters with DnCNN
under identical noise and evaluation protocols.
\item Evaluation across grayscale and color images, multiple Gaussian noise levels,
and salt-and-pepper noise.
\item An analysis of accuracy vs.\ runtime trade-offs and the limits of generalization.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:rw}
reviews classical and learned denoising. Section~\ref{sec:method} formalizes the
noise models, the classical baselines, the DnCNN architecture and training
objective, and the evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:exp} presents
quantitative and qualitative results and a detailed discussion, and
Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:rw}
% ~2 pages. Group by: spatial filters (Gaussian/median/bilateral \cite{tomasi1998}),
% non-local methods (NLM \cite{buades2005}, BM3D \cite{dabov2007}),
% learned methods (DnCNN \cite{zhang2017}, and successors). Cite the BSD dataset \cite{martin2001}.
\textbf{Local spatial filters.} The earliest denoisers replace each pixel with a
weighted average of its neighborhood. Gaussian filtering uses fixed spatial
weights and suppresses noise but blurs edges. The median filter, a non-linear
rank-order operator, is particularly effective against impulse (salt-and-pepper)
noise because extreme values are rejected by the median. The bilateral filter
\cite{tomasi1998} improves edge preservation by weighting neighbors with both a
spatial and an intensity-range kernel, so pixels across strong edges contribute
little.

\textbf{Non-local and transform-domain methods.} Buades \emph{et al.}\
\cite{buades2005} introduced Non-Local Means (NLM), which exploits the
self-similarity of natural images by averaging pixels with similar surrounding
patches anywhere in the image rather than only nearby ones. BM3D
\cite{dabov2007} extends this idea by grouping similar patches into 3-D stacks
and applying collaborative filtering in a transform domain; it remains a strong
baseline and is often regarded as the ceiling of classical denoising.

\textbf{Learning-based methods.} Zhang \emph{et al.}\ \cite{zhang2017} proposed
DnCNN, which reframes denoising as residual regression: a deep CNN predicts the
noise component, leveraging batch normalization and residual learning for
stable, accurate training. A single ``blind'' model can handle a range of noise
levels. Subsequent work has explored deeper, attention-based, and
transformer architectures, but DnCNN remains a clear and widely used
representative of the learned paradigm. Our test images come from the Berkeley
Segmentation Dataset \cite{martin2001}, and SSIM \cite{wang2004ssim} is used
alongside PSNR for perceptual evaluation.

\section{Proposed Approach}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Noise models}
We synthesize additive white Gaussian noise $n \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ for
$\sigma \in \{15,25,50\}$ and salt-and-pepper noise at 5\% density.

\subsection{Classical baselines}
We evaluate five representative classical methods, ordered by increasing
modeling power.

\textbf{Gaussian filter.} A linear convolution with a Gaussian kernel,
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}(p) = \sum_{q\in\mathcal{N}(p)} G_{\sigma_s}(\lVert p-q\rVert)\, y(q),
\qquad G_{\sigma_s}(d)=\tfrac{1}{Z}e^{-d^2/2\sigma_s^2},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}(p)$ is a local window and $Z$ a normalizing constant. It
attenuates high-frequency noise but blurs edges indiscriminately.

\textbf{Median filter.} A non-linear, rank-order operator that replaces each
pixel by the median of its neighborhood,
$\hat{x}(p)=\operatorname{median}\{y(q): q\in\mathcal{N}(p)\}$. Because the median
ignores extreme values, it is highly effective against impulse (salt-and-pepper)
noise.

\textbf{Bilateral filter.} An edge-preserving extension of Gaussian filtering
that weights neighbors by both spatial and intensity proximity,
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}(p)=\frac{1}{Z_p}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{N}(p)}
G_{\sigma_s}(\lVert p-q\rVert)\,G_{\sigma_r}(|y(p)-y(q)|)\,y(q),
\end{equation}
so that pixels across strong edges (large intensity difference) contribute
little, preserving structure.

\textbf{Non-Local Means (NLM).} Exploits image self-similarity by averaging over
all pixels weighted by patch similarity \cite{buades2005},
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}(p)=\sum_{q} w(p,q)\,y(q),\quad
w(p,q)\propto e^{-\lVert P(p)-P(q)\rVert_2^2 / h^2},
\end{equation}
where $P(\cdot)$ denotes a patch around a pixel and $h$ controls filtering
strength; we set $h=\sigma$.

\textbf{BM3D.} Groups similar patches into 3-D stacks and applies collaborative
filtering (hard-thresholding then Wiener filtering) in a sparsifying transform
domain \cite{dabov2007}. It is widely regarded as the strongest classical
denoiser and serves as our upper-bound baseline.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
We report Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Structural Similarity (SSIM)
\cite{wang2004ssim}. For images in $[0,255]$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PSNR}(x,\hat{x}) = 10\log_{10}\frac{255^2}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i (x_i-\hat{x}_i)^2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SSIM}(x,\hat{x}) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_{\hat{x}}+c_1)(2\sigma_{x\hat{x}}+c_2)}
{(\mu_x^2+\mu_{\hat{x}}^2+c_1)(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_{\hat{x}}^2+c_2)},
\end{equation}
where higher PSNR (dB) and SSIM (closer to 1) indicate better reconstruction.
We additionally report average per-image inference time.

\subsection{DnCNN with residual learning}
DnCNN predicts the noise residual $\mathcal{R}(y)\approx n$ and recovers
$\hat{x} = y - \mathcal{R}(y)$. The network stacks Conv--BN--ReLU blocks
(17 layers, 64 feature maps). We train for \emph{blind} denoising by sampling
$\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}(0,55)$ per patch, minimizing
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert \mathcal{R}_\theta(y_i) - (y_i - x_i)\rVert_F^2 .
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Extract $40\times40$ patches from Train400 (flip/rotate augmentation)
\FOR{each epoch}
  \FOR{each minibatch $\{x_i\}$}
    \STATE sample $\sigma$, form $y_i = x_i + n_i$
    \STATE $\hat{n}_i \leftarrow \mathcal{R}_\theta(y_i)$;\; update $\theta$ on $\lVert \hat{n}_i-n_i\rVert^2$
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}

\section{Experimental Analysis}\label{sec:exp}
\textbf{Setup.} Training on Train400; testing on Set12/BSD68 (gray) and Kodak
(color). Metrics: PSNR, SSIM, average inference time. Implemented in PyTorch on a
Colab T4 GPU.

DnCNN is trained for 50 epochs on $40\times40$ patches extracted from Train400,
with Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$ halved every 10 epochs, batch size 128, and
blind noise $\sigma\sim\mathcal{U}(0,55)$. Classical baselines use OpenCV
($5\times5$ Gaussian, $3\times3$ median, bilateral with $d{=}9$, NLM with
$h{=}\sigma$) and the \texttt{bm3d} package. Color images are denoised
per-channel for the learned model.

\subsection{Discussion}
\textbf{Accuracy ranking.} Across all three noise levels and on color images, the
ordering is consistent: BM3D $>$ DnCNN $>$ NLM $>$ simple local filters
(Gaussian/median/bilateral). BM3D leads our blind DnCNN by roughly $0.7$--$1.0$~dB
(e.g., 29.99 vs.\ 29.23~dB at $\sigma=25$). This is expected for two reasons:
(i) BM3D is a highly engineered non-local prior that is widely regarded as the
classical ceiling; and (ii) our DnCNN is trained in the harder \emph{blind}
setting (a single model for $\sigma\in[0,55]$) for only 50 epochs, whereas
noise-specific training and longer schedules in the original work
\cite{zhang2017} report higher numbers. Both BM3D and DnCNN clearly outperform
local filters, and the gap widens as $\sigma$ grows.

\textbf{Accuracy vs.\ speed.} The picture changes entirely when runtime is
considered (Table~\ref{tab:time}). DnCNN denoises a grayscale image in
$\approx0.06$~s versus $\approx11$~s for BM3D---about $185\times$ faster---and on
color the gap reaches $\approx200\times$ (0.44~s vs.\ 87.5~s) because BM3D is
applied per channel. NLM is also far slower than the learned model. Thus DnCNN
trades a sub-1~dB accuracy deficit for one-to-two orders of magnitude lower
inference cost, which is decisive for real-time or large-scale use.

\textbf{Generalization across noise type.} The salt-and-pepper experiment
(Table~\ref{tab:sp}) exposes a key limitation of learned models. The median
filter, whose rank-order operation discards impulse outliers, reaches 31.16~dB,
while our DnCNN---trained only on Gaussian noise---drops to 22.52~dB and even
trails Gaussian filtering. This demonstrates that a data-driven denoiser is only
as general as its training noise distribution, whereas classical operators carry
explicit, transferable assumptions.

\textbf{Color vs.\ grayscale.} The same ranking holds on color Kodak images,
confirming the conclusions are not specific to grayscale. Applying the grayscale
DnCNN per channel is a simple but effective color baseline; a natively
color-trained model, or denoising only the luminance channel in YCbCr, could
further improve color fidelity.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}
We presented a unified, reproducible comparison of five classical denoisers and a
deep DnCNN under identical noise and evaluation protocols, on grayscale and color
images and across Gaussian and salt-and-pepper noise. BM3D remains the strongest
method in PSNR/SSIM and our blind DnCNN follows within about 1~dB, but DnCNN is
one-to-two orders of magnitude faster at inference, making it the most practical
choice when speed matters. The salt-and-pepper results highlight that learned
denoisers do not generalize beyond their training noise distribution, where
classical operators such as the median filter still excel. Future work includes
noise-specific and longer DnCNN training, natively color-trained models, real
camera-noise datasets (e.g., SIDD), and modern transformer-based denoisers.
